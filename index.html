CWRU Nritya is Case Western Reserve University's premier Indian classical dance team with expertise in Bharatanatyam, Kuchipudi, Kathak, and Odissi. From staging productions with live orchestras and choreographing for wedding sangeets to supporting campus philanthropy events like Spartanthon, Nritya is a vibrant part of the community. We take pride in performing across Cleveland, from the Cultural Gardens to the Cleveland Museum of Natural History, with pieces that portray meaningful stories from Hindu mythology, historical events, and contemporary themes.

This past year, Nritya made our return to the national competition scene after several years and placed 3rd at Motor City Dhamaal.

Naadam is CWRU's premier Indian Classical Music club that celebrates Carnatic and Hindustani music styles. The mission for Naadam is to create an established space where students who learn and appreciate Indian Classical Music can express their cultural heritage. We spread awareness of Indian culture and foster an understanding of Indian Classical Music among the Case Western Reserve University community.

Over the past few years, Naadam has grown from having living room concerts to large scale Baithaks and productions including Navarasa Anubhava for the first time last year. We are glad to have gained many talented musicians and are very excited to present Itihasa Anubhava this year!

The Styles

Carnatic Music

Carnatic Music, or Karnātaka Sangeetham, is the traditional music style of South India (Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, and Kerala). It has its roots in the Sāma Veda, which is one of the world's first examples of modal music. While instruments play an important role, most performed pieces are lyrical and emphasize vocal music. Carnatic music evolved greatly from the 14th century onwards, when composers like Sri Purandara Dāsa, Sri Thyāgaraja Swami, Sri Muthuswami Deekshitar, and Sri Syāma Sāstri greatly increased the breadth of repertoire while still adhering to traditional aesthetics. Lyrical topics are greatly rooted in Hindu spirituality, and this art form is used to retell the Sanskrit stories and morals of the Purānas (stories) and Ithihāsas (history) in more colloquial languages like Telugu, Tamil, Kannada, and Malayalam.

Hindustani Music

Hindustani Classical Music is a traditional music system that developed in the northern regions of India. Its roots trace back to the ancient Samaveda, often called the "melody Veda," where music was originally used in devotional and ritual contexts. Over time, it evolved significantly, especially during the Mughal period, when it absorbed influences from Persian musical traditions. At its core, Hindustani music is highly improvisational, allowing performers to creatively explore each raag and its unique emotional expression. Each raag is designed to evoke specific rasas, or moods, and is often associated with specific times of the day, making the performance deeply expressive and personal. A major emotional focus in this tradition is shringar rasa, the expression of love and beauty, often connected to devotion toward deities like Krishna, whose stories frequently inspire compositions. This musical tradition is also deeply connected to classical dance forms like Kathak and Odissi, where dancers interpret the same ragas through intricate footwork, gestures, and storytelling, often bringing Krishna's playful and divine narratives to life through movement.

Bharatanatyam

Bharatanatyam is one of India's oldest classical dance forms, with origins dating back over 3,000 years to the Nāṭya Śāstra, an ancient text by Sage Bharata. Known as the "Fifth Veda," this sacred art is deeply rooted in spirituality, with Lord Nataraja revered as the divine father of this dance form. Originally performed in South Indian temples as an offering to the gods, Bharatanatyam combines bhāva (expression), rāga (melody), tāla (rhythm), and nāṭya (storytelling) to create a powerful visual narrative. Dancers convey emotions and tell stories through hand gestures (mudrās) and facial expressions (abhinaya).

Many of the dancers in Nritya have trained since childhood, developing a strong foundation in technique, expressions, and the spiritual essence of the art. Tonight, you will witness intricate footwork, fluid hand gestures, and expressive facial movements as our dancers bring stories to life through their performance.

Kathak

Kathak is a North Indian classical dance form that tells stories of gods and goddesses, rooted in the tradition of Kathakars — storytellers who conveyed narratives from Hindu epics through dance, music, and song. The term Kathak comes from the Vedic Sanskrit word Katha, which means "story", and Kathakar, which means "the one who tells a story." Originally, wandering Kathakars performed in temples, using dance as a devotional art to depict tales of Hindu deities like Krishna and Shiva. Over time, Kathak evolved in the Mughal courts, where it absorbed the Persian influences of Urdu ghazals (poetry).

Today, Kathak is the only Indian classical dance form that fuses Hindu and Persian artistic traditions, balancing footwork, spins, and storytelling. Dancers wear ghungroos (ankle bells) to accentuate and highlight the intricate rhythms of their movements.

Kuchipudi

Kuchipudi originated in Andhra Pradesh, its roots dating back to the Nāṭya Śāstra (written in 2nd century BCE). Named after Kuchipudi, a village in AP, this dance was originally performed by Bhagavathalu (male dancers/singers/performers) who would travel from village to village and perform these dance-dramas. The modern development of Kuchipudi can be owed to Siddhendra Yogi, a famous poet whose inspiration for his work was said to come from Lord Krishna himself in his dream. With inspiration from the Nāṭya Śāstra as well as temple dances, he composed Bhamakalapam, a well known Yakshaganam, which he asked the Bhagavathalu to premiere. This tradition continued until Vedantam Lakshminarayana Sastri introduced female dancers and solo performances to the art form, revitalizing it into what Kuchipudi is today. Consisting of nritta (thiillanas, jathiswarams), nritya (padams, shabdams, javalis, tarangam) and natya (parinayams, kalapams, daruvus, and other yakshaganas) as retellings of daily life or epics of gods, Kuchipudi also has a few unique components i.e., Vachika Abhinaya — where the dancer speaks to the audience (famously in Bhamakalapam) and Tarangam — where the dancer dances on a brass plate, signifying complete unity between self and Bhudevi.

Odissi

Odissi dance is one of the oldest documented dance styles in India, with roots traced from ancient cave carvings at Udayagiri and Khandagiri to temple sculptures in Odisha dating back to the 9th century. The current form of Odissi dance originated in the Jagannath Temple in Puri, where temple dancers known as Maharis offered their devotion. They would dance in the morning with just a drummer and sing at night, combining hand gestures with storytelling and lyrical movement. Young boys known as Gotipuas joined them later and carried the tradition forward, introducing dynamic and acrobatic elements while preserving its grace. Today's performance draws from the Mahari lineage, particularly the style of Adiguru Pankaj Charan Das, reflecting the origins of Odissi as a living prayer.
